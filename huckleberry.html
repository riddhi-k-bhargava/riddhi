How Huckleberry could track the household,
not just the baby.

What if the person holding your baby right now could see everything they need, without your password? A nanny, a grandparent and both parents all sign into the same account, so the log can't say who gave the medicine or when. That's the promise to parents. For Huckleberry, it's the household it has never owned.

The opportunity

One baby, four caregivers, one login.

Huckleberry's help center answers the sharing question directly: everyone signs into the same account on each phone. That is the workaround, and there is nothing underneath it. Most of the complaints about the calendar, the medication log and the reminders trace back to the same place. The account is the thing the app identifies, so the app has no idea that people exist. Give it people and the rest becomes buildable.

01Nobody can say who did what. Four adults share one account, so every entry lands anonymously. A log that can't name who made an entry also can't warn the next person that the medicine already went in.
02The calendar only shows what already happened. SweetSpot predictions never make it onto the timeline. Every bottle is drawn as a flat fifteen minutes whether it took four or twenty, and nothing tells you whether it was finished. What a caregiver arriving at two o'clock needs is the next four hours.
03The calendar just says "medication." Not which one, not the dose, not when the next is due. Reminders run on a single interval, so two prescriptions on different schedules can't both be right.
04There is nowhere to put a tablet. Care happens in a kitchen, and a screen on the counter is the obvious answer. Without caregiver identity the tablet has no idea who is touching it, so everything it records is anonymous all over again.
What's in it for the parents

The parent who holds it all in her head.

The parent who owns the account is usually the one who knows the whole schedule, and she is the reason it works. That is also the problem. When she is the only one who knows, she can't be away from her phone.

01Everyone gets their own way in. Send a link, pick a role, revoke it in one tap when the babysitter stops coming. Nobody has to hand over a password that also opens their billing.
02Every entry has a name on it. Tap it and see who logged it, from which device, and what got edited afterwards. Asking "did anyone give her the amoxicillin" stops being a text message you send from a meeting.
03The calendar shows what's coming. Planned and actual on one timeline: the SweetSpot window as a band you can see, real feed durations, how much of the bottle actually went down, and each medication by name and dose.
04A tablet on the counter, for whoever is here. Next up, a countdown, four big buttons. No password, no phone, and a hard stop before a second dose goes in.
What's in it for Huckleberry

Seats it is already giving away, and a graph nobody can copy.

01Every shared password is a seat nobody is paying for. One subscription covers four adults and a camera. Charging per caregiver turns the busiest households into the highest paying ones, and an invite link sent to a nanny is a signup that arrives already knowing why it's there.
02Better attribution means better predictions. SweetSpot is only as good as the log underneath it, and multi-caregiver households produce the messiest logs in the system. The families most likely to pay are getting the worst version of the feature they're paying for.
03The household is the part nobody can copy. Nap prediction is copyable, and Nanit did it in 2025 with NextNap, built on camera-measured sleep rather than what a parent remembers to type in. Who cares for this child, on what schedule, with what authority, is much harder to lift. It also fixes the integration problem. Once caregivers are first class, a Nanit camera signs in as one and writes sleep into the same log, with the same attribution, as the nanny does.

Getting there

Migrating a million accounts without inventing a single fact.

Changing what the app identifies is the hard part, and history is the hardest bit of it. Every event ever logged came from one anonymous account. There is no way to work out after the fact who did what, and guessing would break the exact thing the feature is for.

One rule holds the whole migration together: never put a name on an event you didn't witness. Leaving it blank is fine. Filling it in with a good guess means the record is wrong somewhere and nobody knows where.
Phase 0Silent backfill. Every existing account becomes a household of one, with the account holder as Owner. Historical events go to a system actor called "logged before caregivers" rather than to the owner, because the owner may not have been the person who logged them. Nothing changes for the user.Ships dark, and the measure of success is that nobody notices.
Phase 1Detect, then ask. Distinct device IDs on one account already show which households are shared. Use that to target the invite prompt, something along the lines of "looks like three devices use this account", but never to assign an identity to anyone. Detection is allowed to drive the funnel. It is not allowed to write attribution.Measured on invite-prompt conversion by detected device count.
Phase 2Claim one device at a time. The shared password keeps working. Each device that signs in gets a one-time "who's using this device?" chooser that turns it into a member. Migration happens per device, at a moment the person was already opening the app, never as an account-wide event.Guardrail: no forced logouts, and no login wall in front of someone already signed in.
Phase 3Retire the shared credential. Once most active devices are claimed, the shared login stops accepting new devices while existing sessions carry on. Owners get a one-tap invite flow for anything still unclaimed, plus an ownership-transfer path for the households where the nanny is the one holding the login.Gated on claim rate rather than a launch date.
Phase 4Show the seam. The calendar draws a visible marker where caregiver attribution starts. Everything before it stays unlabeled. Smoothing over that line would be trivial to build and would quietly undo the reason anyone trusts the log.Attribution-unknown rate gets tracked, and should fall close to zero within two months of a household converting.

What could break it

Four real risks, with the guardrails that hold them.

Risk 01 · Trust

A nanny is an employee.

A timestamped activity log of a paid worker is workforce monitoring, whatever color the app is. Get it wrong and caregivers experience the feature as being watched rather than being helped.

Mitigation. Consent at invite acceptance, written in plain language. Strict data minimization, so a caregiver sees the child's care record and never billing or another caregiver's location. And an exit they can trigger themselves, revoking their own access without asking anyone.
Risk 02 · Safety

The model states a dose.

A summary that invents a medication name, dose or time is the worst thing this system can produce, and it is the failure an eval score of 99% does nothing to protect you from.

Mitigation. Handle it in the data model, not with a score. Medication lines are rendered straight from the record, so the model writes the narrative around them and never has the authority to state a dose. Paired with dose-lock: a hard stop and a second confirm when someone else gave a medicine recently.
Risk 03 · Dependency

Nanit has no reason to help.

There is no public Nanit API, and since NextNap shipped they compete on nap prediction rather than partner on it. A strategy that assumes an integration may be waiting on a door that never opens.

Mitigation. Build on Apple HealthKit and Google Health Connect as a neutral layer instead of a bilateral deal, and ship a manual "confirm this sleep session" fallback so the feature degrades to zero integrations instead of breaking.
Risk 04 · Monetization

Charging for the safety feature.

Seats are the obvious business model, and the obvious version of it puts attribution behind the paywall. "Pay us and we'll tell you who gave your baby the medicine" is a headline waiting to happen.

Mitigation. Attribution, revocation and the audit trail ship free, because charging for them is what writes that headline. Charge for the parts that save people time: the kiosk surface, handoff briefs, per-medication schedules and device sync. Seats stay strictly additive so no parent has a reason to downgrade.
Validate first. Two questions come before any build: does attributed coverage actually beat the shared-login baseline, and does a per-device claim flow convert without leaking users. A twenty-household diary study answers the first. A held-out control on Phase 2 answers the second. See it in action: rb-mocks-huckleberry-household.lovable.app
